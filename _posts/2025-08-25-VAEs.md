title: "Deriving the ELBO in VAEs"

In generative AI, we frequently wish to sample from high-dimensional distributions. By this, I mean probability distributions over data of some dimension $x \in R^d$, where $d$ is quite large (let’s say, image data where $d=1024\times1024=1,048,576$ - almost as big as my screen time :) ). Sampling from high-dimensional probability distributions is a pain. I won’t go into the details of this here, but it’s a well-documented pain. The natural thing to think of next is, “can we work with something of a lower dimension, some $m << d$, and still figure out how to sample from the high-dimensional distribution?” This brings up a lot of other questions, like "what exactly should this 'something' of a lower dimension be?" and "what objective function can we minimize to do this?"


In order to sample from the distribution of the variable of interest (representing our data), $x$, we wish to find a suitable sampling strategy and parameterization for $p(x)$. We’ve already decided this alone is too rough, so we introduce a new variable, $z \in R^m$. Sadly, this secret lower-dimensional variable that we’ve conjured up is just that - something which we’ve introduced to make learning easier, but has no meaning or connection to our dataset beyond the meaning that we impose on it through this factorization. So, we must figure out what a useful distribution of our lower-dimensional variable $z$ should be.

To make things easier, let’s confine the problem of finding a good distribution for z. We know that z must depend on our dataset x (since we’re using it to get samples which look like our high-dimensional data). We also probably want something that is tractable, or easy to sample from - the whole point of generative AI is to find a tractable strategy for sampling from otherwise intractable high-dimensional data distributions. 

Gaussian distributions are usually the go-to for these kinds of tasks. They’re easy to sample from (good for tractable sampling strategies), we know how to write the form they take as a function (good for ELBO derivations - see below), and they ultimately are parametrized by just two vectors (good for training). So, our possible latent distribution belongs to a family of Gaussians, $q_{\phi}(z)$ (or $q_{\phi}(z|x)$, which is called an *amortized posterior* - a posterior with a shared parametrization for all $x$ instead of having a different learned $q_{\phi}(z)$ for each $x$). This $q$ is parametrized by some vector $\phi$, which contains the mean and standard deviations. Choosing a particular $\phi$ gives us a particular member of our Gaussian family - and in fact, this family is pretty big (infinite, since there are infinitely many $\phi$ we could choose!). We want to optimize for which member of the family can best assist us in our search for a tractable way to approximately sample from $p(x)$. 


We still don’t know what $q_{\phi}(z|x)$ looks like, beyond it being Gaussian and depending on $\phi$. In machine learning, we like having something we can optimize - some function of $\phi$ where, if we can minimize it, we will have found a representation of $q_{\phi}(z|x)$ which pushes $p(x|z)$ to being close to the true $p(x)$ (which we can only measure on data samples).

Basic probability tells us that the joint distribution of these two variables is $p(x,z) = p(x|z)p(z)$. So, we sum out the $z$ to turn our joint probability p(x,z) into a probability distribution $p(x)$. This is called marginalization. As summing and integration are equivalent in the limit (minus some important measure-theoretic details), we get:

$$
p(x) = \int p(x|z) p(z) dz
$$

As a related note, please keep in mind the following fact:

$$
\int Q(z) P(z) dz = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim Q(z)} \left[ P(z) \right].
$$

Now, let's take a natural logarithm of $p(x)$ (which is called the *log-likelihood*)) and see if any fun math falls out. Let's also impose a parametrization $\theta$ over $p(x|z)$ and call it $p_{\theta}(x|z)$ to make this part learnable, as well. In particular, we want something in terms of our *data samples* (or *evidence*).

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \ln p(x) &= \ln \int p_{\theta}(x|z) p(z) dz \\
    &= \ln \int p_{\theta}(x|z) p(z) \cdot \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)} dz \hspace{1cm} \textup{multiply by 1}\\
    &= \ln \int q_{\phi}(z|x) \cdot \frac{p_{\theta}(x|z)p(z)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)} dz \hspace{1cm} \textup{rearrange}\\
    &= \ln \left[ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \frac{p_{\theta}(x|z)p(z)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)} \right ] \hspace{1cm} \textup{using fact from above} \\
    &\geq \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \ln \left[ \frac{p_{\theta}(x|z)p(z)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)} \right] \hspace{1cm} \textup{using Jensen's inequality, since $\ln$ is concave}\\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \ln  p_{\theta}(x|z) + \ln p(z) - \ln q_{\phi}(z|x) \hspace{1cm} \textup{log rules} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \ln \left [ p_{\theta}(x|z) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \ln \left [ \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{p(z)} \right ] \hspace{1cm} \textup{log and exp rules} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \ln \left [ p_{\theta}(x|z) \right] - KL \left( q_{\phi}(z|x) \| p(z) \right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line employs the fact that
$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P(x)} \ln \left( \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right) = \textup{KL}\left( P(x)\| Q(x) \right),
$$
where $\textup{KL}(\cdot \| \cdot)$ is the Kullback-Liebler divergence, a powerful, always non-negative, notion of distance (although not symmetric!) between two probability distributions which is frequently used in generative modeling.

If we look at the first term, $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_{\phi}(z|x)} \ln \left [ p_{\theta}(x|z) \right]$, we see that first, the data $x$ was *encoded* through our $q_{\phi}(z|x)$. Then, latent samples $z$ were drawn from $q_{\phi}(z|x)$. Finally $x$ was *decoded* from its latent representation $z$. The second term quantifies the distance between our encoder and the latent distribution, and can be interpreted as regularizing the latent space.

This gives us an Evidence Lower-Bound Objective (ELBO) - in other words, *a function (objective) in terms of our data (evidence) which we can optimize to improve our latent representation.* This is a lower-bound because we employed Jensen's inequality. This means that if we maximize the ELBO, we will never *overestimate* the true log-likelihood - however, we certainly might *underestimate* it, which wouldn't be so good either. Now, let's play with the log-likelihood again and see how bad this underestimation could be:

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \ln p(x) &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left [ \ln p(x) \right ]\\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left[ \ln \left( p(x) \cdot \frac{p(z|x)}{p(z|x)} \right)\right] \hspace{1cm} \textup{multiply by 1} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{p_{\theta}(x|z)p(z)}{p(z|x)} \right) \right]  \hspace{1cm} \textup{since $p(x|z)p(z) = p(z|x)p(x)$} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{p(z|x)p(z)}{p_{\theta}(x|z)} \cdot \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)}\right) \right]  \hspace{1cm} \textup{multiply by 1 and replace with $p_{\theta}(x|z)$ parameterization} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left[ \ln \left( p(z|x) \cdot \frac{p(z)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)} \cdot \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{p_{\theta}(x|z)}\right) \right]  \hspace{1cm} \textup{moving things around} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left[ \ln \left( p_{\theta}(x|z) \right ) + \ln \left( \frac{p(z)}{q_{\phi}(z|x)} \right) + \ln \left( \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{p(z|x)}\right) \right]  \hspace{1cm} \textup{log rules} \\
    &= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left[ \ln \left( p_{\theta}(x|z) \right ) - \ln \left( \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{p(z)} \right) + \ln \left( \frac{q_{\phi}(z|x)}{p(z|x)}\right) \right]  \hspace{1cm} \textup{more log rules} \\
    &=\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q(z|x)} \left [ \ln p_{\theta}(x|z) \right ] - \textup{KL} \left( q_{\phi}(z|x) \| p(z) \right) + \textup{KL}\left( q_{\phi}(z|x) \| p(z|x)\right ).
\end{aligned}
$$

I want to emphasize that there was a goal at the back of my mind when doing all of the above. We wanted to bring back the ELBO that we found earlier, but we also wanted a way to quantify *the gap* between the ELBO and the true-log-likelihood (which is why we avoided using any inequalities here, unlike we did earlier when we were just trying to derive the ELBO). We clearly see that the first two terms are the ELBO we found earlier, and since we did make use of any inequalities in this derivation, we know that the *exact difference* between the ELBO (what we can optimize directly) and the true $\ln p(x)$ (what we *can't* optimize directly) is given by $ \textup{KL}\left( q_{\phi}(z|x) \| p_{\theta}(z|x)\right )$.

To summarize, we've done some important things:

- We have created a (stochastic, or probabilistic) encoder $q_{\phi}(z|x)$ and decoder $p_{\theta}(x|z)$, which are ultimately just parametrizations of conditional distributions between our latent variables $z$ and data $x$.
- We have a lower bound (the ELBO) on the log-likelihood $\ln p(x)$ which we are trying to model.
- We know exactly what the gap is between the ELBO and the log-likelihood.

There's a bit of a subtlety to 2 - while we do have an ELBO in terms of $x$, we still have an expectation, which we haven't gotten rid of. Right now, we'd have to pass in many (stochastic) samples to get a reasonable approximation of the prior and an estimation of the expectation, which could have quite a bit of variance in and of itself. This would not be very easy to optimize over. A technique called the reparameterization trick can help us here. I'm generally pretty against the term "trick" as a pedagogical thing, since at least the "tricks" I've seen (mostly in fields like real and complex analysis) tend to present themselves as if they come out of nowhere, and strongly prefer terms like "technique" which suggest something that can be honed. For the sake of consistency with the rest of the literature (and because I think this particular "trick" is reasonably intuitive), I'll keep calling it the "reparametrization trick." 

Suppose you have a way to sample from a standard Gaussian, $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and a simple four-function calculator. I'm not happy with this, and I tell you, "I don't care about a standard Gaussian! I want you to give me a bunch of samples from a Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^{2}$!" It turns out you have all you need! As long as you can sample from $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, you can give me samples from the distribution I cared about by simply computing $z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \varepsilon$ and handing me the $z$ samples.

This is the "reparametrization trick." Since we know that the encoder is coming from a family of Gaussians and can thus be written as $q_{\phi}(z|x) = \mathcal{N}\left( z| \mu_{\phi}(x), \sigma^{2}_{\phi}(x)\right)$, our latent \texit{samples} can be drawn as

$$
z = \mu_{\phi}(x) + \sigma_{\phi}(x) \cdot \varepsilon, \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
$$
but our ELBO optimization can simply take place over the Gaussian distribution parameters $\mu_{\phi}(x)$ and $\sigma^{2}_{\phi}(x)$.

So, the task of the encoder is now just to learn the *parameters* of the latent distribution, instead of trying to learn to generate samples of the conditional latent distribution. We've completely removed the stochasticity (which is entirely contained in the variable $\varepsilon$) out of the optimization objective, which can help combat gradient explosion and improve the stability of our optimization. Also, because the latent distribution is Gaussian, everything about the latent distribution can be reparametrized nicely into something in terms of a *standard* Gaussian, which makes expectations and integrals tractable and removes any stochasticity from sampling from the optimization of the ELBO.

All together, this is allows us to train a variational autoencoder (VAE)! We have an encoder $q_{\phi}(z|x)$ which takes us into a lower-dimensional latent space with a standard Gaussian shape, a decoder $p_{\theta}(x|z)$ which takes us from the standard Gaussian latent space to the generated data, and an objective function to optimize the likelihood in terms of $\phi$ and $\theta$ (which we can parametrize as a neural network). The sampling process would then be as follows: sample from the lower-dimensional prior $z \sim p(z)$, and then follow it through to the decoder $x \sim p(x|z)$.

This is probably one of the simplest cases of a VAE. In general, the flow is still the same. To conclude, in this post, we have derived the ELBO of a VAE with Gaussian amortized latent posterior, noted that the error between the log-likelihood and the ELBO is precisely a KL-divergence between the learned and true prior, and discussed the "reparameterization trick" we can use to tractably estimate and optimize the ELBO.
